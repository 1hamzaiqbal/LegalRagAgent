Legal RAG should work well for legal question answering: many answers turn on
statutes, regulations, court holdings, or compact doctrinal rules, and
retrieval can put that text in front of a language model \citep{lewis2020rag}.
The hard part is often the first query. A bar-exam fact pattern may mention a
driver, crutches, and a banana peel, while the support passage states a
well-established rule about proximate cause. A housing question may name a
state, while a national corpus that mixes statutes from every state can
return a plausible-sounding rule from the wrong jurisdiction. Final answer
accuracy alone hides these failures.

A common fix is to let the model first \emph{write} the kind of passage it
expects to find and search with that text instead of the raw question.
HyDE asks the model for a short hallucinated authority --- a synthetic
passage in the corpus's voice --- and embeds that text for retrieval
\citep{gao2023hyde}. Query expansion and rewriting use related
generated search text \citep{wang2023query2doc}. The idea is attractive
in law because a model can often name the legal category behind a fact
pattern before retrieval sees the corpus. The same methods can also fail by
abstracting away anchors, choosing the wrong legal frame, or retrieving
plausible but irrelevant authority.

We introduce \hyre{}, a fixed two-call generated-query method that mirrors
expert legal reasoning. A practitioner facing a fact pattern forms a quick
intuition about what is at issue, looks up the controlling authority by that
intuition, and then judges the evidence on its own merits rather than
anchoring on the first guess. \hyre{} encodes the same loop. The first
model call writes two objects: a private draft answer (the ``snap''
intuition) and a separate legal search passage in the style of the
authority that would resolve the question. Only the search passage is
embedded for retrieval; the draft is not handed forward. The second call
answers the original question from the retrieved legal text, judging the
evidence without anchoring on its own first guess. The name reflects this
structure: ``snap'' is the quick private draft answer, and HyRE abbreviates
Hypothetical Reasoning Embeddings, the legal passage embedded for retrieval.

We report a two-benchmark study chosen for opposite ends of the
question-corpus vocabulary gap. BarExamQA is the weak-query regime:
fact-pattern questions describe accidents, transactions, and procedures in
narrative prose, while the support corpus uses doctrinal vocabulary
(``proximate cause,'' ``hearsay exception,'' ``adverse possession''). The
raw question and the relevant rule passage barely share words. HousingQA is
the strong-query regime: statutory-entailment questions ask directly about
landlord and tenant terms (``Can a landlord evict a tenant for committing
waste?''), so the question vocabulary already overlaps with the housing
statutes that contain the answer.

\hyre{} helps in proportion to that gap. On BarExamQA it improves raw-question
RAG by +2.4\pp{}, +4.0\pp{}, and +5.1\pp{} for Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 4 26B, and
Llama 3.3 70B; it raises BarExamQA Hit@5 from 1.4\% to 9.5--12.1\% for the
three generators; and it matches or exceeds the labeled gold passage on the
two larger models. On HousingQA, where raw queries are already corpus-shaped,
\hyre{} delivers answer parity rather than lift. Pooled across all five
signed model-dataset cells, \hyre{} averages +1.1\pp{} over raw-question RAG
and is at parity with HyDE.

We define \hyre{} as a fixed two-call legal RAG method, compare it against
raw-question RAG, HyDE, and oracle evidence controls on BarExamQA and
HousingQA, and report when the method helps by tracking both answer accuracy
and whether gold evidence appears in the top five retrieved passages.
Structured query rewriting and additional diagnostics are in the appendix.
